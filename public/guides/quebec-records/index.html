How to find your grandparents’ records in Quebec

Quebec has some of the best-kept family records in the world. Here is where they are and how to search them, one step at a time.

1. Start with what your family already knows

Before you search anything, write down what you have: full names, rough birth years, the parish or town, and the names of spouses. Ask the oldest relatives first, and look for prayer cards, wedding invitations and obituaries in family drawers. One known marriage is often enough to open up several generations.

Tip: In Quebec records, women usually appear under their maiden name all their life. Search for your grandmother under the name she was born with, not her married name.

2. Understand parish registers

For most of Quebec’s history, births, marriages and burials were recorded by the parish as baptisms, marriages and burials. Registers were kept in two copies, one for the parish and one sent to the civil authorities, which is why so many have survived fires and floods.

A Quebec marriage record usually names the bride’s and groom’s parents. That is what lets you climb back one generation at a time: each marriage gives you the next couple to look for.

3. Search the official archives

  • Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du Québec (BAnQ) holds civil copies of parish registers, notarial records, court records and old newspapers. Many collections can be searched online, and its archive centres across Quebec can help in person.
  • Library and Archives Canada has the national censuses, immigration records and military service files.
  • FamilySearch is free to use with an account and has scanned images of many Quebec Catholic parish registers.

Some popular indexes of Quebec registers are sold by subscription services. You can often find the same record free on the archive sites above; it may just take a little longer to browse.

4. For recent records, ask the civil registrar

Recent births, marriages and deaths are confidential and are not online. Copies and certificates are issued by the Directeur de l’état civil du Québec, usually only to the person named or to close family, with proof of identity.

5. Use the census to place a family in time

Canadian censuses list everyone in a household with ages, origins and religion. They are released to the public 92 years after they are taken. Use them to check that the family you found in a register is the right one.

6. Write down where each fact came from

Note the parish, the date and the archive for every record you find. When two records disagree, which happens often, the source tells you which one to trust.
